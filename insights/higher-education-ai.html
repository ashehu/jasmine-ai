Why college? The question universities must answer in the age of AI

Students need credible proof that a university will prepare them for a future changing faster than the traditional degree cycle.

JasmineAugust 2026Perspective

The new question

Students are no longer choosing only between universities.

A prospective student today is choosing between a four-year degree, a shorter credential, immediate employment, online learning, entrepreneurship, and pathways that may not have existed a few years ago.

They are making that decision while artificial intelligence changes how people write, design, research, analyze, program, and communicate. The question is no longer only, “Which university should I attend?” It is increasingly, “Should I go to university at all, and will the degree still be valuable when I graduate?”

That concern is moving into the mainstream. In 2026, 46% of Americans told Gallup that AI would make college degrees less important over the next five years, compared with 20% who believed degrees would become more important. Cost and workforce preparation were also among the leading reasons people expressed limited confidence in higher education. Gallup, 2026

The enrollment challenge AI does not automatically make higher education less valuable. It raises the burden of proof.

International pressure

The decision becomes even more complex across borders.

International students and their families are weighing tuition, distance, safety, visa requirements, employment prospects, cultural belonging, and the future value of a U.S. degree.

The United States hosted approximately 1.2 million international students during the 2024–2025 academic year. Yet new international enrollment fell 7% during that year, and an early snapshot for fall 2025 reported a 17% decline in students beginning at a U.S. institution for the first time. Graduate enrollment was especially affected. Institute of International Education, Open Doors 2025

Those numbers do not prove that any single policy caused the decline. They do show a more fragile recruitment pipeline. Changes in screening and vetting can add another source of uncertainty: the State Department, for example, requires online-presence review for student and exchange-visitor visa applicants. U.S. Department of State guidance

A polished campus video cannot answer what it feels like to arrive from another country, find a community, learn in a second language, secure an internship, or prepare for an AI-affected profession. Those questions require credible messengers.

The value story

Universities must demonstrate adaptability, not predictability.

Students want to know whether the curriculum is adapting quickly enough. They want to see how AI is being used inside real courses, laboratories, internships, and research projects. They want evidence that graduates will be able to work with AI rather than compete blindly against it.

The World Economic Forum reports that employers expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030. At the same time, employers continue to value analytical thinking, resilience, leadership, collaboration, and other human capabilities alongside technical AI skills. World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025

The U.S. Department of Education has described a dual role for postsecondary institutions: using AI to improve access and student success while preparing students for a rapidly changing career landscape. U.S. Department of Education, 2025

01Tool fluency

Use powerful AI systems productively without surrendering judgment or responsibility.

02Durable thinking

Evaluate evidence, recognize uncertainty, connect disciplines, and form original conclusions.

03Applied experience

Turn knowledge into work through research, studios, laboratories, internships, and real projects.

04Human capability

Develop communication, leadership, collaboration, ethics, resilience, and contextual understanding.

05Adaptive networks

Build relationships with faculty, employers, researchers, alumni, and peers that continue beyond graduation.

These ideas cannot remain buried in strategic plans and curriculum documents. Prospective students need to see them in practice.

The messengers

A university is experienced through people, not one institutional voice.

A professor may be the best person to demonstrate how an AI curriculum is changing. A recent graduate may be most credible when explaining how the degree translated into work. A current international student may describe the support system with a specificity that an admissions brochure cannot.

01Current students

Show the real academic, creative, and social experience rather than a generic picture of campus life.

02International students and alumni

Address transition, belonging, support, learning, and career preparation through direct experience.

03Faculty and researchers

Explain how programs, projects, assessments, and fields of study are evolving.

04Recent graduates

Connect coursework and university relationships to the early-career reality.

05Employers and practitioners

Describe the combination of technical and human capabilities their organizations need.

06Parents and counselors

Help families evaluate affordability, safety, student support, and confidence in the decision.

The objective is not to turn every student or professor into an influencer. It is to identify the people who can responsibly answer a particular question for a particular audience.

The campaign

Ask the question directly: What is a degree worth in the age of AI?

The campaign should not answer with reassurance alone. It should show evidence.

A professor could demonstrate how an established course has been redesigned. A student could show an AI-assisted project and explain what human judgment the work still required. An alumnus could describe how their responsibilities changed after AI entered the workplace. An employer could explain which capabilities became more valuable, not less.

Promise 01You will learn how to work with AI.
Promise 02You will develop capabilities AI cannot replace easily.
Promise 03You will apply your learning in real settings.
Promise 04You will join a network that continues beyond graduation.

That is more credible than claiming that a particular career is “AI-proof.” No institution can responsibly guarantee which jobs will exist four years from now. It can demonstrate how it prepares students to adapt.

The guardrails

Recruitment intelligence must protect student trust.

Jasmine should not infer immigration status, disability, ethnicity, religion, financial vulnerability, or other sensitive characteristics. Audience analysis should rely on appropriate aggregated signals, declared campaign requirements, and legally permitted criteria.

The system should never generate promises about admission, visas, scholarships, employment, or salary outcomes. University teams must approve claims, disclosures, accessibility, and creator activation. International offices and legal counsel should review content involving visa or employment rules.

The responsible role for AI Organize the evidence, expose uncertainty, and help people make a better decision. Do not make the institutional decision for them.

A practical pilot

Start with one program and three markets.

An institution does not need a university-wide transformation to test this approach. A focused pilot can create a measurable decision while respecting institutional review.

Higher education does not need to pretend that AI has made the future predictable. Students will recognize that as marketing. Universities need to show that they are paying attention, adapting deliberately, and preparing students to exercise judgment in a world of powerful tools and continuous change.
